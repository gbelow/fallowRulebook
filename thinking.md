# product workflow - how to turn this into a game

## Phase 0 - fill the gaps

### 1. Finish the core game

*** I am here ***

Flag everything from the core that needs work

Freeze and dont touch anything else after freezing 

**Freeze protocol (the loophole, answered):**
- The resolution core loop has been frozen for a long time. What changes is content (magic, tools, knowledges) and integration with morale/chase. Content is reworkable at any time — the freeze governs the core only.
- Breaking the core freeze is allowed only for playtest-discovered core failures — never for new ideas, polish, or itch.
- Every core freeze break must be written up publicly as a revision post. The change pays for itself as content. This is extra work, so it self-limits: you can't do it obsessively the way you've changed core before.
- Test for "nearly done": the open items (knowledges, comfort, social-combat wiring, magic integration) are all content/integration — none touch the resolution core in combat.tex. If a proposed change touches the resolution core, that's a red flag, not a fix.

### 2. Create a quickstart

1. stripped character creation
2. combat + 1 subsystem (chase)
3. 3-5 pages of pre-gens - monsters, one point crawl map
4. Created quick reference material/ Tables of rules and guidelines
5. Strategy guide
6. Online tool - make it sufficient for playtest

This must be a teachable quickstart. Make it more friendly than the reference style you have. 
PS. remember that you got praised by your short and precise style before! just add worked examples and visual aids

### 3. External Playtest

Test the speed claim
Test the sizes
Test the feel
Test the pitches - win public for the test
At least 3 groups who never sat at my table. Tool doubles as telemetry — timing data retires the speed insecurity with evidence.

### 4. Transform the reference into a game book (after freeze, before/with quickstart)

- Teaching layer lives in the quickstart, NOT the core book. Core book stays reference-style; add only worked examples per chapter and an example-of-play transcript (2 pages of a real fight resolving).
- Content layer: bestiary minimum, sample adventure, sample NPCs with stat blocks, one filled-out point crawl.
- Setting decision — Opinionated. Setting suggestion may exist. Periods between antiquity to pike-and-shot. Sample adventure is labeled a demonstration, not canon.
- Tutorials and adventures are the most churn-expensive artifacts. None of this starts before the freeze holds.

## Phase 2

A. Crowdfunding - email list > 1000

B. Direct sales first - if the list is still under ~1000 when the book is done

Reality check on A: 1x goal ≈ break even. Net is typically 5-15%. Profit lives in overshoot, late pledges/PDF sales, and the leftover print run sold at cons/direct. This is a slow game with a livelihood secured elsewhere — no deadline until I pull the trigger.

No timeline for phases 0 and 1 — they run in parallel once the quickstart exists. Design essays start now, before the quickstart; everything else in phase 1 waits for artifacts.

## Phase 3 - Campaign Prep

- Art budget is the big line item — a cover plus ~15–20 interior pieces is the minimum viable visual pitch
  for a historical/gritty game. Commission 2–3 pieces before the campaign; they're your ad creative.
  - Campaign page structure mirrors your pitches: lead with magic-optional, demonstrate
  lethality-with-agency, embed the actual-play video, show a combat turn resolving fast on camera.
  - Line up 10–20 "day-one amplifiers" (other designers, newsletter writers, YouTubers who cover indie
  TTRPGs) and tell them a month early.
  - Budget 10% of the goal for post-campaign surprises. Always.

## How to talk about it

#### Safe Pitches - Design values (unfalsifiable — publish now, freely)

1. Verisimilitude as a design goal. 
2. "Lethal but never arbitrary" as a philosophy. 
3. Magic that obeys physics. 
4. "Every rule answers another." 
5. The cheap/impact/hard-to-avoid triangle. 

These are positions, not performance claims.

#### Empirical claims (falsifiable — stay quiet until you have evidence).

 "Combat resolves fast." 
 "Chases work at the table." 
 "Low-skill characters remain a threat." 
 "The math stays in your head." 
 
 These are exactly the claims you're insecure about — Don't publish them yet. 
 When your frozen-core playtests and the tool give you data, these become your strongest content precisely because they were earned. 

Secondary hooks: classless skill-based characters; real medieval gear behavior (plate deflects, weapons
  play like their historical counterparts); modular toolkits (war, horror, intrigue, survival) letting one
  book serve many campaigns.

## Work Rythm

  1. Start with value essays, ~monthly. Triangle rule, magic-obeys-physics, why DLs are judgment calls not
  lookup tables, why your game has real chase rules. No promises, no dates, no "coming soon" pressure — just
  positions with your name on them. Slow-game compatible.
  2. Let resonance be your market research. Which essay gets discussed tells you which pitch leads the
  quickstart and campaign page. You're auditioning your pitches in public, cheaply.
  3. Publish failures and revisions in the same voice. Lab notebook, not marketing.
  4. Convert empirical claims to content only as evidence arrives — timed combat runs, tool telemetry,
  playtest anecdotes. By launch, every empirical pitch on your campaign page should trace to something
  demonstrable, ideally the browser tool.
  5. Keep a claims ledger — a one-page list of every claim and its evidence status (believed / playtested /
  demonstrated). Any public statement about the game checks against it. This is cheap discipline and it's
  exactly the habit that prevents the "overpromise then cringe" arc.
